# Negotiation framework

The right offer is anchored to **specific defects and verified costs**,
not to "I'd like to pay less." A defect-anchored offer is hard for the
seller to argue with because each dollar off has a receipt behind it.

## The math

Start with **KBB private-party Fair value** for the verified trim and
mileage. This is the price the seller can reasonably expect.

Subtract:

- Cost of every defect found on the PPI, at the shop's quoted price
- Cost of every overdue scheduled service (timing belt, transmission
  fluid, coolant, spark plugs)
- Cost of every consumable near end-of-life (tires older than 6 years,
  12V battery older than 5 years, brake pads under 4mm)
- Negotiation cushion: $200 to $500 for "the seller has to feel they
  got somewhere"

Add nothing for things the seller volunteers as positive ("new tires,"
"recent service"); those are already in KBB's value.

The result is your **walk-away offer**.

## KBB methodology notes

- Use the **private-party** value, not retail or trade-in. Dealers
  price retail because they offer warranties and financing; a private
  seller is not entitled to retail.
- Use **Fair** condition, not Good or Excellent. Most 10-year-old cars
  are Fair. "Good" requires no significant cosmetic issues and full
  service records. "Excellent" basically never applies to a private-
  party car.
- Verify the **trim** from photos before pulling KBB. Trim affects KBB
  value by $500 to $3,000. See `trim-id-guide.md`.
- KBB Fair is typically the seller's reasonable "asking" target.
  Edmunds True Market Value tends to run higher; CarGurus instant offer
  tends to run lower (it's wholesale).
- If KBB shows a wide range for the year/mileage/trim, mid-range Fair is
  the anchor.

## The conversation

Frame the offer around the defects, not the price.

**Good script:**

> "Hey, I really like the car and I think it's been well-maintained. The
> PPI flagged a few things: front pads are at 3mm, tires are 7 years old,
> and the coolant has never been flushed. The shop quoted $1,400 to do
> those three items. I also noticed the timing belt is due in about 5K
> miles, which is another $900. I can do $X today, cash, picked up
> Saturday."

This works because:

- Each dollar off is tied to something the seller cannot dispute
- "I really like the car" signals a real buyer, not a tire-kicker
- "Today, cash, picked up Saturday" is a concrete commitment

**Bad script:**

> "Will you take $X?"

This is a lowball. It puts the seller on the defensive and invites a
counter that splits the difference.

## Sizing the offer

Three tiers based on PPI outcome and how interested the user is:

**Tier 1: PPI clean, user is committed**
- Offer KBB Fair minus 5% to 10%
- Counters anchored to small consumables (battery age, tire age)
- Expect to land at KBB Fair to KBB Fair minus 5%

**Tier 2: PPI shows real but fixable defects, user is interested**
- Offer KBB Fair minus the sum of defect costs minus $500 cushion
- Be ready to come up to KBB Fair minus defect costs
- Walk if seller won't budge from sticker

**Tier 3: PPI shows serious issues, user is on the fence**
- Either walk, or offer well below market and mean it
- Do not split the difference. The seller is not your friend; the
  defect is real.

## Things that are NOT negotiation levers

- "I saw it cheaper online" (the seller will say "go buy that one")
- General market conditions
- Your budget (the seller doesn't care)
- Asking for a discount because you're paying cash (cash is just cash;
  it's the norm for private-party sales)

## Things that ARE negotiation levers (besides the PPI)

- **Days on market.** Use FB Marketplace's "[N] weeks ago" timestamp
  on the listing. 4+ weeks at the current price = motivated seller.
  Stale listings discount well.
- **Recent price reductions.** If the seller has already lowered the
  price once, they will lower it again.
- **Pickup logistics.** Offering to handle title transfer paperwork or
  pick up on the seller's schedule has value.
- **Inspector-flagged items the seller didn't disclose.** "You said
  the AC works, but the shop found a leak in the condenser." Now you're
  not negotiating; you're correcting a misrepresentation.

## When the seller won't move

If the seller refuses to budge from sticker after a defect-anchored
offer, two interpretations:

1. They genuinely don't know the defects exist (uneducated seller). The
   car will sit on the market. Walk and re-message in two weeks.
2. They have another buyer (or claim to). Sometimes true, often a tactic.
   Walk politely. If it's real, they'll close with the other buyer; if
   it's a bluff, they'll be back in your inbox in a week.

There are always more cars. The user's leverage is willingness to walk.

## Closing

When the seller accepts:

1. Confirm the price in writing (FB messages count; screenshot).
2. Bring exact cash (or cashier's check from a bank the seller agrees
   to meet at).
3. Verify the title at meet-up: VIN matches the car, name matches the
   seller's ID, no liens, no brands.
4. Sign the title and a bill of sale (most states have a template).
5. Note the odometer reading on the bill of sale and title.
6. Get a receipt.

If anything at meet-up is different from what was agreed (different
title status, different mileage, undisclosed damage), the offer is
withdrawn. Walk.
